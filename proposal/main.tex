\section*{Introduction}
Learners of English rarely get feedback on \emph{which sound} they got wrong: a teacher can listen to only a few
sentences per student, and speaking apps usually return a single sentence-level score. Korean speakers make
systematic errors that are easy to name but hard to self-monitor; in the corpus used here the most frequent
Korean-L1 substitutions are \texttt{DH}$\rightarrow$\texttt{D} (``\emph{the}''$\rightarrow$``\emph{de}'', 357 cases),
\texttt{Z}$\rightarrow$\texttt{S} (296) and \texttt{IH}$\rightarrow$\texttt{IY} (114). The goal of this project is a model that takes a
recording of a read sentence and its expected phone sequence and flags each phone as
\emph{correct} or \emph{mispronounced}. Deep learning fits this task because the same phone is realised very
differently across speakers, genders and recording conditions; fixed acoustic thresholds such as the classic
Goodness of Pronunciation score \cite{witt2000phone} generalise poorly, whereas a CNN can learn the relevant
time--frequency patterns directly from spectrograms.

\section*{Background \& Related Work}
\textbf{GOP} \cite{witt2000phone} scores a phone by the posterior of the expected phone from an HMM acoustic
model and thresholds it; it is still the standard non-neural approach. \textbf{Hu et al.} \cite{hu2015improved}
replaced the GMM with a DNN acoustic model and fed GOP-like features into phone-specific logistic-regression
classifiers. \textbf{Leung et al.} \cite{leung2019cnn} proposed an end-to-end CNN-RNN-CTC phone recogniser
whose output is aligned with the expected phones, removing the need for forced alignment. \textbf{wav2vec 2.0}
\cite{baevski2020wav2vec} learns speech representations from unlabeled audio; \textbf{Xu et al.} \cite{xu2021explore}
and \textbf{Peng et al.} \cite{peng2021study} fine-tuned it for mispronunciation detection and reported strong
results on L2-ARCTIC. \textbf{L2-ARCTIC} \cite{zhao2018l2arctic} is the non-native speech corpus with phone-level
error annotations used in all of these works. In contrast to the large pre-trained models, this project designs a
small CNN trained from scratch and studies how much a weakly supervised pre-training step (phone recognition on noisy forced-alignment labels) on
\emph{unannotated} utterances of the same corpus helps.

\section*{Data Processing}
\textbf{Source.} L2-ARCTIC v5.0 \cite{zhao2018l2arctic} (CC BY-NC 4.0): 24 speakers, 6 L1s (Arabic, Mandarin,
Hindi, Korean, Spanish, Vietnamese; 2 male + 2 female each), $\sim$1,130 read sentences per speaker at 44.1\,kHz.
3,599 of these utterances have manual phone-level annotations. \textbf{Steps.}
(1)~Parse the \texttt{phones} tier of every annotation TextGrid. A label is either a phone (correct) or
\texttt{CPL,PPL,type} with type \texttt{s} (substitution), \texttt{d} (deletion) or \texttt{a} (addition); stress digits
are removed and \texttt{err}/\texttt{*} tags kept. This yields 120,243 phone segments: 101,633 correct, 14,098
substitutions, 3,420 deletions and 1,092 additions (15.5\% errors; Korean speakers 9.9\%).
(2)~Label each expected phone as mispronounced if it is a substitution or deletion; additions have no expected
phone and are excluded. (3)~Resample audio to 16\,kHz and compute 64-bin log-mel spectrograms
(25\,ms window, 10\,ms hop). (4)~Phones are short (median 90\,ms, 95th percentile 200\,ms), so each sample is a fixed
300\,ms window centred on the phone, plus a binary mask channel marking the phone's own frames.
(5)~Split by speaker so no voice appears in two sets: test = two Korean speakers (HKK, YDCK); validation = one
speaker from each other L1; training = the remaining 17 speakers (including the other two Korean speakers).
(6)~For pre-training, repurpose the 23,268 \emph{unannotated} utterances: their forced-alignment TextGrids
give phone boundaries, so every segment becomes a 40-class phone-recognition example.
(7)~Handle the 1:5.5 class imbalance with a class-weighted loss; normalise features with training-set statistics.

\section*{Architecture}
A 2-D CNN encoder takes the $2\times64\times30$ input (log-mel + mask): four blocks of
conv~3$\times$3 $\rightarrow$ batch norm $\rightarrow$ ReLU $\rightarrow$ max-pool (32--128 channels),
followed by global average pooling. It is first trained as a 40-way phone classifier on the unannotated data.
For detection, its 128-d output is concatenated with a learned 16-d embedding of the expected phone and passed
through two fully connected layers with dropout and a sigmoid. Training uses Adam and weighted binary
cross-entropy, with early stopping on validation F1.

\section*{Baseline Model}
Per segment, compute 13 MFCCs with first and second derivatives (39 features per frame), take their mean and
standard deviation over the phone (78 features) and append a one-hot expected phone (40). A class-weighted
logistic regression (scikit-learn) is trained on the same speaker split. Both models are evaluated with
precision, recall and F1 on the \emph{mispronounced} class, the standard metrics for this task, overall and
per L1 on the Korean test speakers.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}
The labels encode one annotation team's judgement of ``standard'' American English; a model trained on them may
treat an accent as an error and discourage intelligible but accented speech, so outputs should be presented as
feedback, not a grade. With only 24 speakers from 6 L1s, all adults reading the same prompts, performance on
other L1s, children, spontaneous speech or other microphones is unknown and likely worse. Voice recordings are
biometric data: the corpus licence is non-commercial and forbids redistribution, so the audio is not included
in the public repository.
